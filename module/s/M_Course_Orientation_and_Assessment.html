Course Orientation and Assessment

This unit covers what this course is for, what you will have in your hands by the end of it, which roles you will simulate, and how the 100% is split.

🎯 Learning objectives

# What you will be able to do The real problem it lets you answer
1 State the two deliverables this course exists to produce — a complete MRD and a complete PRD with wireframes — and say who each document is written for "What do I actually put in a portfolio when I apply for a junior PM job?" — a transcript proves attendance; two real requirement documents prove you can do the work.
2 Distinguish the six product-side job titles (PO, PM, PjM, PMM, product designer, interface designer) that this course asks you to simulate Job ads in Taiwan use these six titles almost interchangeably. If you cannot tell them apart, you cannot tell which ad you are qualified for.
3 Name, for any project you are on, who the Sponsor, Approver, User, Accountable and Consulted people are "Why did my proposal die after everyone said it was a good idea?" — usually because nobody asked the person who could veto it.
4 Judge whether a given product idea is inside this course's scope (content, platform, mini-site) or outside it (e-commerce, tools, journalism itself) Half of all failed team projects fail in week one, by picking something the course cannot help them build.
5 Predict your own grade profile from the five components and decide where to spend effort 65% of your grade comes from work done outside the two pitch days. You can calculate that now, or discover it in week 15.

What this course is for and what you walk out with

  • The course exists so that you can apply for a junior content-product job, and so that a communication-school background becomes an advantage there rather than a handicap.
  • Three concrete outputs: a complete MRD, a complete PRD including wireframes, and a prepared answer set for PM interviews.
  • Everything in the 16 weeks is arranged to feed those three outputs.

The purpose is employability, not vocabulary

Two things justify this course. First, that you can credibly apply for an entry-level content product manager job when it ends. Second, that what you already have from a communication school — audience reasoning, editorial judgement, the ability to write for a reader who is not you — turns into an advantage in product work instead of being left at the door.

Both of those are testable claims about you in sixteen weeks, not promises about the syllabus.

A portfolio beats a transcript in this job market

Nobody hiring a junior PM asks for your grade in this course. They ask what you have written and whether you can defend it. That is why the two documents are the deliverables and the pitches are only the occasions on which you defend them.

The roles you will play and the people you must handle

  • Six product-side titles get used almost interchangeably in job ads; you will simulate them so that you can read an ad correctly.
  • A project also contains eight decision identities, which are a separate axis from job title.
  • Most product failures at your level are not analysis failures — they are failures to identify who could veto the thing.

Six titles, one confusing job market

These are the technical-function roles you will rotate through in your team. They overlap in practice, and different Taiwanese companies draw the lines differently.

Title The one-line difference
Product Owner (PO) Owns the backlog and says what gets built next
Product Manager (PM) Owns the product's success — market, users, and requirements
Project Manager (PjM) Owns schedule, dependencies, and delivery, not the product's direction
Product Marketing Manager (PMM) Owns positioning, launch, and how the product is explained outward
Product Designer Owns how the product works as an experience
Interface Designer Owns how the product looks and behaves on screen

The eight people every project contains

Job titles tell you what someone does. These identities tell you what someone can do to you. They are a different axis, and you need both.

Identity Plain description
Sponsor The person who decides to give you resources
User The person who actually uses the product
Approver The person who can veto it
Recommender The person who decides to start it
Accountable / Decide The person who gets the credit if it works and the blame if it does not
Responsible / Perform The person who does the work
Informed The person who will hurt you if you do not report to them
Consulted / Input The person who gets angry if you do not ask them

The last two are written in that deliberately blunt way because that is how they behave in real organisations. W14 comes back to this with the power/interest grid and RACI.

For students · The two identities students always miss

Almost every student team names the Sponsor and the User correctly and forgets the Approver and the Consulted. Those two are exactly the ones that kill proposals late — a veto arrives in week 15, or a department you never asked declares that you have stepped on their territory. When you list stakeholders, list the ones who can stop you before you list the ones who will love you.

What is in scope, what is not, and how you are graded

  • This course covers content products: content, platforms, mini-sites. It does not cover e-commerce or tool products, and it does not cover reporting and writing.
  • It covers product planning, documents, and communication — and it does include building something.
  • 65% of the grade is earned outside the two pitch days.

In scope: content, platform, mini-site

The products you plan here are content products. A news vertical, a podcast network, a newsletter business, an interactive explainer platform, a small site built for one audience — these are all fair game.

Out of scope: e-commerce, tools, and journalism itself

Three things are explicitly outside this course. E-commerce and tool products, because their unit economics and their user logic are different enough to need their own course. And content production — reporting, interviewing, writing — because you already have courses for that. Here you plan the thing that content lives in, not the content.

The course is planning, documents, and communication — with real building

The three verbs are planning, documenting, and communicating. But this is not a paper course: you will build. Agentic AI (Claude Code) is used as a working partner nearly every week, which is why you are strongly advised to hold a paid Claude plan for the semester — the free tier will not survive the weekly workload.

What you should expect to walk away with

A working vocabulary of the terms. Familiarity with the document types. A stronger pitch. Service-and-product design thinking. And a way of folding what you know about communication into a product rather than bolting it on afterwards.

Every dimension is scored 1–4: 4 excellent (original, rigorous, industry-ready), 3 proficient (meets all stated criteria), 2 developing (partial; gaps in evidence or argument), 1 needs work (superficial; misses core requirements). Full dimension tables are in the syllabus.

AI use is expected; opaque AI use is penalised

You are not being tested on whether you used AI. You are being tested on whether you can say what you asked it, what it got wrong, and why you kept or overrode its answer. Copy-pasted AI output that contradicts your team's own stated reasoning is the one thing that costs marks.

Theory Report rotation. Pairs and trios are drawn today and each signs up for two weeks between W3 and W15. Each report is a 15-minute student-led opening on that week's Primary Theory. The full weekly theory list is in Theory Report Schedule.

AI Skill Sharing slots. Two 5–7 minute slots per person, opened in W2, W4, W7, W10, W11, W13, and W15. First come, first served.

⚠️ Common misconceptions

What students assume Why it is wrong What to do instead
"The pitches are the course, everything else is homework" The two pitches are 45%. Theory reports, skill sharings and participation are 55% — and they are spread across every week Treat the weekly slots as the main grade surface and the pitches as the defence of work already done
"A content product means content I produce" This course plans the container, not the contents. Reporting and writing are out of scope Pick a product where the interesting question is who it serves and how it sustains itself
"If I use AI well I should hide it" AI use is expected. Concealment is what loses marks, because the reflection on what AI got wrong is part of what is graded Keep a record of the prompts that failed; those are worth more in a pitch than the ones that worked
"The team lead does the deciding, so the rest of us just execute" Participation is scored individually, including flip-exercise contribution and red-team quality Own one role publicly and be visibly the person who knows that part best
"PM, PO and PjM are basically the same job" They differ on what they own: backlog, product success, and delivery. Taiwanese job ads blur them, which is exactly why you need to read the duties, not the title When reading an ad, ignore the title and find the sentence that says what you would be accountable for

💬 Discussion and reflection

Pair review

Find a partner and take a project you have actually been on — an internship, a student organisation, a group assignment, a club event.

  1. Name the Sponsor, the Approver, and the User of that project
  2. Name one person who was Consulted, and one who should have been but was not
  3. Say what went wrong because of the person in step 2

Swap and do the same for your partner's project. If neither of you can find a missing Consulted person, you have probably mislabelled the Approver.

Individual reflection

Write one sentence to keep: "Of the five grade components, the one I am most likely to under-invest in is __, and the week I will notice it is ____."

✍️ Take-home task

In no more than 150 words, describe a content product you would like to plan this semester, and state explicitly why it is not an e-commerce product, not a tool, and not a piece of content. Bring it to W3.
