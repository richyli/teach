Jobs-to-be-Done

A short primer on Jobs-to-be-Done: what it claims, where it sits in this course, and the questions it lets you ask about your own product.

Clayton M. Christensen, Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon & David S. Duncan, 2016 — "What job is the customer hiring this product to do?"

Core idea

  • People don't buy products — they hire them to make progress in a specific situation.
  • A "job" has three layers:
  • Functional — the practical task (kill my morning commute boredom)
  • Social — how I want others to see me
  • Emotional — how I want to feel
  • The biggest market is almost always nonconsumption — people stuck without a good hire.
  • Canonical case: the milkshake is hired for a long, one-handed, boring drive — not for taste.

Where this theory sits in this course

  • Supporting theory in W7 User Research & MRD Assembly — optional stretch material, and a useful contrasting lens in those weeks.
  • It is never the week's Primary theory, so no report is assigned to it. It still earns marks when it does real work in a pitch.

Lens questions for your project

  • What situation triggers the hire? (Time, place, mood, social setting.)
  • What was the user doing just before they reached for our product?
  • What did they fire to hire us — and what could fire us next?
  • Are we competing inside our category, or against a nonconsumption alternative?
  • Which dimension dominates — functional, social, or emotional?

Recommended reading

  • Christensen, Hall, Dillon & Duncan (2016) Competing Against Luck — the practitioner book. See Christensen 2016 Competing Against Luck.
  • Ulwick (2005) What Customers Want — Outcome-Driven Innovation, the operational version of JTBD.
  • Bettencourt & Ulwick (2008) "The Customer-Centered Innovation Map," HBR — job mapping technique.
  • Background concept notes: jobs to be done framework, jobs have functional social and emotional dimensions.

Pair with Agentic AI

  • Move 1 — Use AI as a cheap synthetic interviewer to generate JTBD hypotheses before you go into the field.
  • Move 2 — Force the AI to articulate jobs in the user's own language, not in your product vocabulary.
  • Move 3 — Generate the firing alternatives the user might switch to, including AI itself.
  • Pitfall — Confusing what the user says they want with the underlying job. AI amplifies surface-level wording; you still need real situational interviews to triangulate the job.

Discussion

  • Pick one feature on your product's roadmap. What job is it being hired for? Can you state the job without mentioning your company's name?
  • Where is the nonconsumption in your market — the people stuck doing nothing because every existing hire is too expensive, too complicated, or too embarrassing?
  • If a generative AI assistant were a competing "hire" for the same job, what would it have to do to fire you?
