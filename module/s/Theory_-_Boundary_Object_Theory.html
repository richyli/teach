Boundary Object Theory

A short primer on Boundary Object Theory: what it claims, where it sits in this course, and the questions it lets you ask about your own product.

Susan Leigh Star & James R. Griesemer, 1989 — "Objects that travel across communities while keeping coherence."

Core idea

  • A boundary object is an artefact that lives in multiple communities of practice at once.
  • It is ill-structured in common use but well-structured in individual use — flexible at the centre, sharp at the edges.
  • Star & Griesemer (1989) name four types:
  • Repositories — modular catalogues (museums, databases).
  • Ideal types — diagrams or maps abstract enough to travel (an "atlas").
  • Coincident boundaries — same shape, different content (a state border that means different things to different actors).
  • Standardised forms — schemas that constrain what can be exchanged, not how it is interpreted.
  • Works precisely because of interpretive flexibility — it lets each community keep its own meaning while still cooperating.

Where this theory sits in this course

Lens questions for your project

  • What artefacts in our process travel across communities — and which break in transit?
  • Where is the interpretive flexibility load-bearing? (Strip it out and the doc dies.)
  • Which of the four types best describes our PRD: repository, ideal type, coincident boundary, or standardised form?
  • Where have we over-specified and killed the boundary object's job?

Recommended reading

  • Star & Griesemer (1989) "Institutional Ecology, 'Translations' and Boundary Objects," Social Studies of Science 19(3) — the founding paper.
  • Star (2010) "This is Not a Boundary Object: Reflections on the Origin of a Concept," STHV 35(5) — Star's own warning against the concept's misuse.
  • Carlile (2002) "A Pragmatic View of Knowledge and Boundaries," Organization Science 13(4) — extends to syntactic / semantic / pragmatic boundaries in product development.
  • Background notes: boundary object, object fidelity determines boundary spanning effectiveness, prototype mediates knowledge boundary in IS development, boundary objects gain efficacy through sociomaterial performance.

Pair with Agentic AI

  • Move 1 — Have AI translate your MRD/PRD across audiences (engineering / marketing / legal / finance) to surface where the boundary object distorts meaning between communities.
  • Move 2 — Design Claude Skills as boundary objects — clear shared structure plus flexible local interpretation; the skill spec is the standardised form, the invocation is the local content.
  • Move 3 — Use AI to detect over-specification: ask it to find every clause that would break if a stakeholder read it through a different lens.
  • Pitfall — Over-specifying the PRD kills its boundary-object function. Controlled ambiguity is a feature, not a bug — don't let AI tools that promise "no ambiguity" remove the very flexibility that lets the doc travel.

Discussion

  • Pick a recent PRD. Which community did it serve worst — and was that because it was too vague, or too specific?
  • Identify one place where interpretive flexibility in your team's working artefacts is doing real work. What would break if you nailed it down?
  • If AI translates your PRD into four stakeholder-specific versions, who owns the canonical one — and what happens when the versions drift?
