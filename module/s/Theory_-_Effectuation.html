Effectuation

A short primer on Effectuation Theory: what it claims, where it sits in this course, and the questions it lets you ask about your own product.

Saras D. Sarasvathy, 2001 — "Means → ends, not ends → means."

Core idea

  • Causal logic: pick a goal, then assemble means. (Standard MBA planning.)
  • Effectual logic: start with the means at hand, then discover what ends are possible.
  • Five principles:
  • Bird-in-hand — start with who I am, what I know, who I know.
  • Affordable loss — bound the downside, not the upside.
  • Lemonade — leverage surprises rather than avoid them.
  • Crazy quilt — partner with whoever is willing to commit.
  • Pilot in the plane — control the future you can shape; don't try to predict.

Where this theory sits in this course

  • Primary theory in W12 Scope Management — a rotating pair opens those sessions with a 15-minute report on it.
  • Supporting theory in W15 Risk Management — optional stretch material, and a useful contrasting lens in those weeks.

Lens questions for your project

  • What are our bird-in-hand means right now? (People, skills, data, distribution, brand.)
  • What is our affordable loss for this experiment — the worst case we can absorb without harm?
  • What recent surprise could we turn into lemonade instead of treating as a setback?
  • Who has self-selected to partner with us, and are we using them?
  • Are we trying to predict the market, when we should be trying to shape it?

Recommended reading

  • Sarasvathy (2001) "Causation and Effectuation," Academy of Management Review 26(2) — the founding paper. See Sarasvathy 2001 Causation Effectuation.
  • Sarasvathy (2008) Effectuation: Elements of Entrepreneurial Expertise — the book-length treatment.
  • Read, Song & Smit (2009) Journal of Business Venturing — meta-analysis of effectuation evidence.
  • Background notes: Sarasvathy, Saras D, Matalamäki 2017 Effectuation Review, Nielsen Lassen 2012 Identity Effectuation.

Pair with Agentic AI

  • Move 1 — AI dramatically expands means at hand. Before scoping, list every Agentic AI capability you can call upon — your bird-in-hand inventory just grew.
  • Move 2 — Apply affordable loss to AI experiments: what's the worst that can happen if the prototype fails or hallucinates? Bound that, then ship.
  • Move 3 — Treat surprising AI behaviour as lemonade — unexpected emergent capabilities can become the product, if you let them.
  • Pitfall — Using AI to predict outcomes (causal logic) when the situation is genuinely uncertain (effectual logic). AI forecasts are seductive; they make planning feel sound when shaping would serve you better.

Discussion

  • Without naming any goal, list our team's five strongest means. Which ones are we under-using?
  • Pick the most ambitious item on the roadmap. What's the affordable loss for testing it next sprint?
  • Where on the roadmap are we trying to predict when we should be trying to shape — and what would change if we flipped?
