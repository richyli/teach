Vibe Coding a Small Tool

This unit covers writing a three-part spec before any code exists, recognising how AI output fails, and deciding whether a tool you built is worth adopting for real.

🎯 Learning objectives

The point is not to learn to code. It is that someone with no engineering background actually builds and ships a small tool other people can use.

# What you will be able to do The real problem it lets you answer
1 Write a three-part spec — a one-line specification, a not-doing list, and a verification point — and vibe-code a working prototype from it Why does telling an AI to write code straight away usually go wrong? Every gap you leave in the spec gets filled with a default that looks reasonable and is wrong.
2 Recognise how AI output fails — hallucination, invented defaults, fixing A and breaking B, blowing up at the edges — and repair it by opening a clean conversation and asking the AI to audit the work Why does it say everything is fine when you ask in the original conversation, and find a dozen problems when you ask in a fresh one? Because models are bad at grading their own work.
3 Decide whether a finished tool is worth adopting by a team Once you can build it, what is the genuinely hard managerial judgement? Weigh time saved × frequency × number of people against maintenance cost × risk of error. An AI will never volunteer that something is too expensive to keep. That judgement is yours.

Working with AI: the Tekton / Yingzao method

From Claude Build Day Hackathon. This shows how a person drives an AI, not how an AI does your typing.

Plan before you build. The winning team's first piece of advice was to map the whole project before building any of it: write the PRD (see the model in PRD Writing and Stress Test) and 50 Notion tickets first, and only then let the AI start. You control the AI with the spec instead of being led by it.

Do not trust the AI's self-assessment — build external verification in. The PRD states plainly that models score their own work badly, so the verifier ran in a fresh context window and scored independently. A self-correction loop ran until twenty tests passed, across 339 incremental build stages. Designing the check into the process, rather than spot-checking afterwards, is the core discipline.

Use the AI for tool choice and architecture, not just code. From the third-place team, Custom Universe: use Claude to choose tools, not just to write code — bring it into decisions like whether to add Apple RealityKit to the pipeline.

Hands-on

  1. Write a one-line specification, a not-doing list, and one external verification checkpoint for your own small tool
  2. Vibe-code it, then build and ship it
  3. Compare what you get with a spec against what you get without one
  4. Decide whether it is worth adopting for real

✍️ Take-home task

Finish and publish the tool, and write a reflection on what the AI did for you and what you had to supply yourself.

💬 Discussion and reflection

Hands-on showcase

Demo the tool you published and say the one thing the AI did that surprised you most.

Pair review

Take one question together: is this thing worth adopting by a team? Answer it with one criterion — maintenance cost against time saved.

Individual reflection

Write one sentence to keep: "With no engineering background, did I just build something usable? Has that changed who I think can build a product?"
