PM Role and Product Lifecycle

This unit covers the four kinds of product manager, the five stages every content product passes through, and what changes in that job once an AI agent is sitting in the team.

🎯 Learning objectives

# What you will be able to do The real problem it lets you answer
1 Place any PM job on two axes — inbound vs outbound, and technical vs content — and say what the day looks like in each quadrant "Two companies advertise 'Product Manager' and the jobs are nothing alike." They are in different quadrants; the title hides it.
2 Walk a product idea through the five lifecycle stages (research → MRD → PRD → launch → metrics) and name the artefact each stage owes the next one "Why did the engineers build the wrong thing when the market research was fine?" — because nothing carried the research forward into a specification.
3 Use Activity Theory to locate where a task moved from a person to a tool, and what rule or division of labour had to change with it "We gave the AI the drafting work and the team got slower." Something else in the system did not move with it.
4 Distinguish the parts of PM work that an agent genuinely absorbs from the parts that only look absorbable Jared Sires at Anthropic saved 2–3 hours a day with a drafting tool and still could not delegate the judgement about which 700 accounts to call.
5 Judge which lifecycle stage is the riskiest to hand to an AI agent, and defend that judgement against a classmate who picks a different one This is the argument you will have with a real engineering lead in W12, so it is worth losing it here first.

Use an AI as your study partner before class. Paste the prompt below.

Engeström's Activity Theory describes human work as a six-element system: subject, object, tool, rules, community, and division of labor. Take one concrete case: a small newsroom introduces an AI agent that drafts every article summary. Fill in the six elements before the agent arrives, then fill them in again after. Then answer two questions. (1) Which element changed the most, and which one did nobody update? (2) Engeström argues that learning happens when contradictions between elements force the system to expand — name the specific contradiction this newsroom now has. Do not summarise the theory first. Fill in the table, then answer the two questions.

Bring your filled table to class. Compare the "element nobody updated" with two classmates — that column is where most real AI adoption failures live, and you will find that people picked different elements for good reasons.

The spine of this unit: four PM archetypes → the five-stage lifecycle and what each stage hands over → Activity Theory: the team as a system, not a headcount → what an agent actually absorbs → which stage you would never hand over.

Four product managers, one job title

  • PM work splits on two axes: inbound (defining what to build) versus outbound (taking it to market), and technical versus content.
  • The four quadrants have different daily rhythms, different documents, and different bosses.
  • A communication-school graduate starts strongest in the outbound-content quadrant and is most exposed in inbound-technical.

Inbound PMs face the product; outbound PMs face the market

The inbound side is where requirements are made: talking to users, writing the MRD and PRD, arguing about scope, sitting with engineers. The outbound side is where the product meets the world: positioning, launch, pricing narrative, the story sales and marketing repeat. Both are called Product Manager. In a small company they are one exhausted person.

The technical–content axis decides which arguments you can win

A technical PM's authority comes from being able to argue about feasibility with engineers. A content PM's authority comes from being able to argue about audiences, editorial judgement, and what a reader will actually do. You are being trained for the second, in an environment where the first is becoming cheaper to fake.

Your quadrant is your first hire, not your last

Technical Content
Inbound Specs, APIs, feasibility trade-offs, engineer-facing Audience research, editorial requirements, journey design
Outbound Developer relations, technical positioning Launch narrative, partnerships, audience growth

Most first jobs open in the two right-hand cells. The point of the semester is to give you enough of the left-hand cells that you can hold a conversation there.

For students · Reading a Taiwanese job ad

The title tells you almost nothing. Find the sentence that says what you would be responsible for, and see whether it names requirements (inbound) or market (outbound), and whether it names engineers (technical) or audiences (content). One sentence beats the whole title.

Five stages, and what each one owes the next

  • The lifecycle is research → MRD → PRD → launch → metrics, and each stage exists to hand a specific artefact to the next.
  • A stage that produces no artefact has not happened, however much discussion it consumed.
  • Skipping a handover does not save time; it moves the cost into the stage where mistakes are most expensive to fix.

Research owes the MRD an evidenced market and a named user

Market sizing, competitors, segments, and one user job stated in the user's own words. If research ends in "we think young people would like this", the MRD has nothing to be built from.

The MRD owes the PRD a decision about who this is for

The MRD is where the market case is settled: who, how big, against whom, positioned how. W4 to W7 build it and W8 defends it. It is a cross-department document, so it must survive being read by someone who does not want the project to happen.

The PRD owes the build a specification that cannot be quietly reinterpreted

Structure, acceptance criteria, edge cases. This is the document where being vague costs the most, because an under-specified PRD is now filled in by an AI agent with plausible defaults rather than by an engineer who would have come and asked you.

Launch owes metrics a definition of what success would have looked like

If the success measure is written after launch, it will be written to match whatever happened.

Metrics owe research the next question

The loop closes here or it does not close at all. Metrics that nobody turns back into a research question are a report, not a lifecycle.

Activity Theory: the team is a system, and AI moved one element

  • Engeström models work as six elements — subject, object, tool, rules, community, division of labor — that must stay consistent with each other.
  • Adding an AI agent changes the tool element instantly, and the rules and division of labor only if someone deliberately changes them.
  • Learning, in this theory, comes from the contradiction that opens up in between.

The PM is the one who keeps the six elements consistent

A product team is a subject (the team) acting on an object (the product) through tools, inside a community, under rules, with a division of labor. Nobody's job description says "keep these six aligned". That is what a product manager actually does, and it is why the role is hard to describe in a job ad. See Activity Theory.

Agentic AI changes the tool overnight and the rules never

When Claude Code arrives, the tool element changes in an afternoon. Who is allowed to ship what, who reviews whose output, what counts as done — those are rules and division of labor, and they change only when a human decides to change them. The gap between the two is where most AI-adoption pain sits, and Engeström's claim is that the contradiction is not a malfunction; it is the thing that forces the system to expand.

A non-coder became a product manager by productising his own job

Jared Sires joined Anthropic in 2024 as a salesperson who had never written code, doing 10 to 15 customer calls a day and still handling email at 10pm. Using Claude Code he built CLAFTS (a drafting tool inside Gmail, around 4,300 lines, hundreds of system-prompt iterations to match his own voice, saving 2–3 hours a day), a Daily Brief that ranked 700 accounts by usage to decide who to call, and eventually a Sales Plugin that around 80% of the sales team adopted within months. His title moved from salesperson to GTM product manager. Full case: GTM Engineering at Anthropic.

The lesson is not "AI writes code" but "the barrier moved, so who counts as a builder moved"

His own advice was to open the tool, find a task that slows you down, and ask how to build a solution to that one task — not to start from a grand product. That is the same shape as the AI Skill Sharing assignment you signed up for.

What the agent did not absorb

It drafted his email. It did not decide which accounts mattered, whether a deal was real, or what tone a relationship needed. Two supporting theories name this split: Human-AI Teaming on how tasks and authority get allocated, and Distributed Cognition on how the thinking gets spread across people, agents, and artefacts rather than replaced.

An agent changes what a tool can do in an afternoon. It changes who is allowed to decide only when somebody rewrites the rule — and until somebody does, the team is running a system whose elements no longer agree with each other.

Hands-on — Lifecycle Speed-Run

Your team receives a fictional content-product brief — for example, an AI-driven local-news app for elderly readers in Taiwan.

  1. Produce exactly one bullet for each of the five lifecycle stages: research, MRD, PRD, launch, metrics
  2. Use an AI agent for any part of it, and keep a note of what you asked
  3. Make each bullet reference the one before it — if the PRD bullet could stand alone without the MRD bullet, it is not a lifecycle, it is a list
  4. Mark the one stage where your AI output was confidently wrong

Then argue it out: which stage was hardest, and which one would you refuse to hand to an agent? Two teams that pick different stages should say why before anyone concedes.

⚠️ Common misconceptions

What students assume Why it is wrong What to do instead
"PM is the person who decides what to build" In most organisations the PM has no authority to decide and no team reporting to them. Influence comes from having the evidence and the document Notice how often the answer to "who decides" is somebody you have to persuade
"The lifecycle is a checklist you tick through" It is a chain of handovers. A stage with no artefact did not happen Ask of each stage: what does the next stage receive from this one
"Agentic AI mainly saves time" The bigger change is who is allowed to build. Sires was not a faster salesperson; he became a different role Ask what the tool lets a non-specialist attempt, not how many hours it saves
"Activity Theory is just a diagram of a team" Its claim is specifically about contradictions between elements driving change. Without the contradiction it is only an org chart with more boxes Use it to find the element nobody updated, which is where the pain is
"AI can do research, so research is the safe stage to delegate" Research is where a plausible-sounding fabrication is hardest to detect and most expensive downstream, because every later stage inherits it Delegate the collection, keep the judgement about what counts as evidence
