PM Role and Product Lifecycle

This unit covers the five stages every content product passes through, what each stage owes the next one, and which parts of that work an AI agent genuinely absorbs.

🎯 Learning objectives

# What you will be able to do The real problem it lets you answer
1 Walk a product idea through the five lifecycle stages (research → MRD → PRD → launch → metrics) and name the artefact each stage owes the next one "Why did the engineers build the wrong thing when the market research was fine?" — because nothing carried the research forward into a specification.
2 Distinguish the parts of PM work that an agent genuinely absorbs from the parts that only look absorbable Jared Sires at Anthropic saved 2–3 hours a day with a drafting tool and still could not delegate the judgement about which 700 accounts to call.
3 Explain how the barrier moving changes who counts as a builder, not just how fast building goes A salesperson who had never written code became a product manager in eighteen months. That is a statement about the role, not about the tool.
4 Judge which lifecycle stage is the riskiest to hand to an AI agent, and defend that judgement against a classmate who picks a different one This is the argument you will have with a real engineering lead in W12, so it is worth losing it here first.

🤖 Pre-class AI workout

Use an AI as your study partner before class. Paste the prompt below.

Take one real content product you use — a news app, a newsletter, a podcast platform. Reconstruct the five stages its team must have gone through: market research, market requirements, product requirements, launch, and metrics. For each stage, write the one artefact that stage had to hand to the next one, and say how you can tell from the finished product whether that handover actually happened. Then name the stage where you can see the evidence is weakest, and say what in the product gives it away. Do not describe the product's features. Work backwards from the product to the process.

Bring the stage you judged weakest. Most people will name the same one or two, and the reason they are guessable is what this unit is about.

The spine of this unit: the five-stage lifecycle and what each stage hands over → what an agent absorbs and what it only appears to absorb → which stage you would never hand over.

Five stages, and what each one owes the next

  • The lifecycle is research → MRD → PRD → launch → metrics, and each stage exists to hand a specific artefact to the next.
  • A stage that produces no artefact has not happened, however much discussion it consumed.
  • Skipping a handover does not save time; it moves the cost into the stage where mistakes are most expensive to fix.

Research owes the MRD an evidenced market and a named user

Market sizing, competitors, segments, and one user job stated in the user's own words. If research ends in "we think young people would like this", the MRD has nothing to be built from.

The MRD owes the PRD a decision about who this is for

The MRD is where the market case is settled: who, how big, against whom, positioned how. W4 to W7 build it and W8 defends it. It is a cross-department document, so it must survive being read by someone who does not want the project to happen.

The PRD owes the build a specification that cannot be quietly reinterpreted

Structure, acceptance criteria, edge cases. This is the document where being vague costs the most, because an under-specified PRD is now filled in by an AI agent with plausible defaults rather than by an engineer who would have come and asked you.

Launch owes metrics a definition of what success would have looked like

If the success measure is written after launch, it will be written to match whatever happened.

Metrics owe research the next question

The loop closes here or it does not close at all. Metrics that nobody turns back into a research question are a report, not a lifecycle.

For students · Where the person doing this work sits

The lifecycle above is what the job runs across; the taxonomy of what the job is called, and which quadrant of it you are being trained for, is PM Roles. The theory that explains why keeping the whole system consistent is nobody's written job description — and therefore ends up being yours — is Activity Theory.

What an agent absorbs, and what only looks absorbable

  • The tool changes in an afternoon; who is allowed to decide changes only when a person rewrites the rule.
  • A non-coder productised his own job and his role changed, which is a bigger fact than the hours he saved.
  • The agent drafted the work. It did not decide which work was worth doing.

A non-coder became a product manager by productising his own job

Jared Sires joined Anthropic in 2024 as a salesperson who had never written code, doing 10 to 15 customer calls a day and still handling email at 10pm. Using Claude Code he built CLAFTS (a drafting tool inside Gmail, around 4,300 lines, hundreds of system-prompt iterations to match his own voice, saving 2–3 hours a day), a Daily Brief that ranked 700 accounts by usage to decide who to call, and eventually a Sales Plugin that around 80% of the sales team adopted within months. His title moved from salesperson to GTM product manager. Full case: GTM Engineering at Anthropic.

The lesson is not "AI writes code" but "the barrier moved, so who counts as a builder moved"

His own advice was to open the tool, find a task that slows you down, and ask how to build a solution to that one task — not to start from a grand product. That is the same shape as the AI Skill Sharing assignment you signed up for.

Personal tool → validated → productised → distributed is a lifecycle in miniature

He proved the value on himself first, adopted within 24 hours of a Slack post, then packaged the winners into a company-wide plugin designed for a new hire to install on day one. That is the same five stages at a smaller scale, which is why it is worth studying in week one.

What the agent did not absorb

It drafted his email. It did not decide which accounts mattered, whether a deal was real, or what tone a relationship needed. Two supporting theories name this split: Human-AI Teaming on how tasks and authority get allocated, and Distributed Cognition on how the thinking gets spread across people, agents, and artefacts rather than replaced.

An agent changes what a tool can do in an afternoon. It changes who is allowed to decide only when somebody rewrites the rule.

Hands-on — Lifecycle Speed-Run

The situation. A local-news app for readers over 65 in your home town. The news is AI-summarised, the type is large, and the whole thing is run by one part-time editor. That is the brief your team just received.

  1. Produce exactly one bullet for each of the five lifecycle stages: research, MRD, PRD, launch, metrics
  2. Use an AI agent for any part of it, and keep a note of what you asked
  3. Make each bullet reference the one before it — if the PRD bullet could stand alone without the MRD bullet, it is not a lifecycle, it is a list
  4. Mark the one stage where your AI output was confidently wrong

Then argue it out: which stage was hardest, and which one would you refuse to hand to an agent? Two teams that pick different stages should say why before anyone concedes.

⚠️ Common misconceptions

What students assume Why it is wrong What to do instead
"The lifecycle is a checklist you tick through" It is a chain of handovers. A stage with no artefact did not happen Ask of each stage: what does the next stage receive from this one
"Agentic AI mainly saves time" The bigger change is who is allowed to build. Sires was not a faster salesperson; he became a different role Ask what the tool lets a non-specialist attempt, not how many hours it saves
"AI can do research, so research is the safe stage to delegate" Research is where a plausible fabrication is hardest to detect and most expensive downstream, because every later stage inherits it Delegate the collection, keep the judgement about what counts as evidence
"Launch is the end of the lifecycle" Launch owes metrics a definition of success, and metrics owe research the next question. A loop that does not close is a report Write the success measure before launch, not after

In short

  • Each stage of the lifecycle owes the next one a specific artefact; that is what makes it a lifecycle rather than a list of phases.
  • Skipping a handover does not save time, it relocates the cost to the stage where fixing it is most expensive.
  • What agents absorbed is the execution, not the judgement about which problem was worth solving — which is the part of the role you are being trained for.

💬 Discussion and reflection

Pair review

The situation. A weekly newsletter for students job-hunting in media: what it covers, who it is for, how it would survive past ten issues. If your team finished a Speed-Run sheet in class, use that product instead; if not, use this one.

Swap sheets with another team.

  1. Find the weakest of their five bullets and say which later stage it would break
  2. Find one bullet that does not actually reference the one before it
  3. Give them one specific rewrite, not a general comment

Most weak bullets will be metrics or research. Say out loud which one yours was.

情境：一份給正在找媒體工作的學生看的每週電子報——它報什麼、給誰看、出到第十期之後靠什麼活下去。課堂上有完成 Speed-Run 表的就用自己那個產品，沒有的話就用這個。

請與另一組交換表格：

  1. 找出對方五條裡最弱的一條，說出它會在後面哪一階段爆掉
  2. 找出一條沒有真的接上前一條的
  3. 給對方一個具體的改寫，不要給籠統的評語

最弱的那條通常是指標或研究。把你們自己弱在哪一條講出來。

Individual reflection

Write one sentence to keep: "If I were the PM of this product, the first stage I would hand to an AI agent is __, and the first thing I would check by hand afterwards is ____."
