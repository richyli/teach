Case Study — Who Is Anime Actually Made For? (Revenue Structure and Isekai Saturation)

個案：日本動畫到底是做給誰看的？

Who this is for: 2026 Product Planning Syllabus W3 (Content Product) as the primary slot, with reusable material for W4 (Market Research) and W15 (Risk Management / Pre-mortem). Pairs with Content Products and Affordance Teardown, Market Research and AI Desk Research, and 風險管理與 Pre-mortem. How to teach it: Not a lecture case. Hand out the causal chain with the last link missing, let teams argue for a cause, then reveal the funding structure. The point lands only if students first commit to a wrong answer. One-line positioning: Every student can name a content product they think is "getting worse." This case shows them that the question "why is this product like this?" is almost never answered at the product layer. It is answered by asking who writes the cheque before anyone has seen the thing.

⚠️ Evidence boundary — read this before teaching. The source is a Japanese trade essay by 大貫佑介 (Yusuke Onuki), CEO of Bushiroad Move, published in Toyo Keizai Online on 2026-08-29 while promoting his own book on the anime business. Two headline numbers carry the whole argument: "roughly 90% of late-night anime revenue depends on overseas platforms" and "average production cost of ¥350M per 12–13 episode season." Neither is given a source, a sample definition, or a year in the original text. This is an insider's account, not a measurement. Do not present either number as an established fact. The recommended move is the opposite: make the missing provenance part of the lesson (see Teaching Move 4). The mechanism the essay describes is widely echoed in industry commentary; the specific magnitudes are not verified here.

Why this case earns a slot

W3 teaches affordance, value proposition, and AI-native unit economics on products students can open in a browser. The risk of that framing is that students learn to explain a product entirely from inside the product. This case breaks that habit with a domain every student already has opinions about, and where the inside-the-product explanation is demonstrably wrong.

It is also unusually clean pedagogically: the phenomenon is visible without any research (open any streaming service's new-arrivals row), the two intuitive explanations are both wrong, and the real explanation is a financing structure students have never been asked to look at.

The facts, as the source states them

Numbers below are the author's assertions. Where he gives no source, this document says so.

The phenomenon. Streaming new-arrival rows and bookstore shelves are saturated with works tagged isekai (reincarnation into another world), clustered around a small keyword set: 転生 (reincarnation), チート (overpowered / "cheat"), 追放 (exiled from the party), 悪役令嬢 (villainess), スローライフ (slow life).

Two intuitive explanations, both rejected by the author. (1) Creators have run out of ideas. (2) Japanese viewers genuinely want this much isekai. The essay's structure is an explicit elimination of both before the third is introduced.

Cost and timing. A one-cour late-night anime (12–13 episodes) costs on average around ¥350 million in total production, and the majority of that is spent before broadcast begins. Production is therefore a forward-investment business: over ¥300M is committed before a single viewer reaction exists. (No source given for the figure.)

Where the money now comes from. The old model recovered cost through domestic disc sales (DVD / Blu-ray); fans buying expensive physical media funded the show. That "disc bubble" is over. Subscription streaming (Netflix, Crunchyroll, Disney+, Amazon Prime Video) replaced it. The author states that about 90% of the current late-night anime revenue structure depends on overseas platforms, centred on North America. (No source given.)

The instrument: MG. Overseas platforms compete for distribution rights before broadcast by bidding a Minimum Guarantee (MG), an upfront payment. For the production company, MG is revenue fixed before production, in some cases covering a substantial share of the budget. If actual viewership passes the guaranteed threshold, revenue share is layered on top. The project that can command a high MG is therefore "the safe and profitable project."

What the greenlight meeting actually asks. Per the author, the dominant question is not "how much will Japanese audiences like this" but "how much MG will an overseas distributor pay for this." Alongside it: which market, which audience, where it gets talked about, music and merchandise fit, social-media virality.

Why isekai specifically. The structural premise, a weak protagonist is thrown into another world, gains overwhelming power, and overturns an unjust world by force, is instantly legible in any country. It requires no knowledge of Japanese school culture or the tacit read-the-room interpersonal register. North American demand skews toward Hollywood-style legible catharsis. Isekai scores high on both legibility and broad-stroke satisfaction, so it commands high MG; high MG means more of it gets made.

Why narou-kei source material. Works originating on 小説家になろう (Shōsetsuka ni Narō), a free web-novel posting site where reader response is made visible as public rankings, arrive with numbers already attached: ranking position, cumulative page views. Against an original pitch's "this should probably do well," a narou-kei pitch says "this many people have already read it." Facing a ¥350M investment decision, the author argues the choice is obvious.

The emerging limit. Most highly-ranked narou-kei works have already been adapted. With the whole industry hunting the same category of "genres that command a price overseas," competition for adaptable IP has intensified into what the industry calls the 原作枯渇問題 (source-material depletion problem): not enough works left that anyone wants to adapt.

Teaching Move 1 (W3) — The payer is not the user, and the product shows it

Run this before any framework. Write the chain on the board with the middle link blank:

visible symptom:  streaming new-arrivals row saturated with one genre
        ↑
selection rule:   ??? decides which projects get greenlit
        ↑
cost structure:   ¥350M committed before any audience feedback exists

Teams fill in the blank and defend it for five minutes. Typical answers: audience demand, algorithmic recommendation, risk-averse producers, cheap animation. Then reveal: the buyer of the distribution right, bidding before the product exists.

The teaching point is not the anime fact. It is that in a content product, "the user" and "the payer" can be entirely different populations, and when they diverge, the product converges on the payer. Students should leave able to ask, of any content product: who pays, at what moment, and on what evidence?

Direct hook into the module's Value Proposition Canvas segment: run the canvas twice for the same anime, once with the Japanese viewer as customer, once with the overseas platform buyer as customer. The two canvases produce different products. That divergence is the case.

Teaching Move 2 (W3) — Unit economics when feedback arrives after the money

Content Products and Affordance Teardown closes on AI-native unit economics: cost per generation against willingness to pay. That framing assumes cost and revenue are roughly contemporaneous. Anime is the opposite regime, and it is the regime most media projects actually live in.

Put the two side by side on the board:

AI-native content product Late-night anime
When cost is incurred per use, after launch almost all before launch
When demand is observed immediately, continuously after the money is gone
Risk instrument usage caps, pricing tiers MG (pre-committed licence fee)
Who absorbs the downside the operator the platform that bid the MG
What the instrument optimises for retention per marginal cost predictability of the pre-sale price

The idea to name out loud: MG converts an unknowable post-hoc quantity ("will audiences like it") into a priceable pre-hoc quantity ("what will a distributor pay"). Any mechanism that performs this conversion will reshape the product toward whatever the pricing party can evaluate in advance. Students should be able to name one such mechanism in their own W16 product proposal.

Teaching Move 3 (W4) — Buyer power, and the market you research is not the market you serve

Reusable in Market Research week. Run 5 Forces on the anime production studio and the buyer-power cell dominates everything else: a small number of global platforms, bidding pre-production, against fragmented studios with sunk costs and no alternative recovery channel now that discs are gone. The essay is, in effect, a first-person account of what maximal buyer power feels like from the supply side.

Second point, sharper: a studio doing "market research" on Japanese viewers would be researching the wrong population. The addressable market for the product and the addressable market for the revenue have separated. Ask teams to check their own W4 market brief for this failure mode. Is the market you sized the market that pays you?

Teaching Move 4 (W3 / W4) — Interrogate the number before you use it

Do not skip this. Give students the essay and ask them to mark every quantitative claim and its source. They will find two load-bearing numbers and zero citations, in a piece written by a senior industry figure launching a book on the topic.

Questions to run:

  • 90% of what, exactly? All anime, or late-night TV anime only? Revenue, or profit? Per title, or industry aggregate? In which year?
  • ¥350M average across which population of titles? An average is fragile where the distribution is skewed, and this one certainly is.
  • The author has a commercial interest in the argument being striking. Does that make it wrong? No. But it changes how much weight it can carry on its own.
  • What would we need in order to verify it? (Point students at the Association of Japanese Animations' annual アニメ産業レポート as the standard reference series.)

This is the honest version of "AI-assisted desk research." The failure mode is not that AI invents facts. It is that a plausible, well-told, uncited number from a credible-sounding source gets laundered into a slide deck and then into a decision. The case is a live specimen.

Teaching Move 5 (W15) — The depletion was foreseeable

For Pre-mortem week. Give teams only the mechanism (fund only what already has demonstrated demand; demonstrated demand is visible on one ranking site) and ask them to predict the failure mode three years out, before revealing 原作枯渇問題.

Most teams get there. That is the point: the depletion is not an external shock, it is the arithmetic of the selection rule. A rule that funds only pre-validated demand stops funding the pipeline that produces pre-validated demand, so the stock drains faster than it refills. Then ask the harder question: at what point does IP acquisition cost rise enough to erase the risk discount the rule was buying in the first place?

Discussion questions

  1. The author rejects "creators ran out of ideas" and "audiences want this." Is his elimination of the second one actually supported? Overseas demand is audience demand. Whose audience counts, and who decided that?
  2. Isekai is described as low-context and therefore exportable. Name a content genre that is high-context and travels badly. What financing structure would have to exist for it to get made at scale?
  3. If ranking position and cumulative PV are what convince investors, what does an author on that platform rationally optimise for? What happens to the works that would have been written under a different incentive?
  4. MG shifts downside risk to the platform. What does the studio give up in exchange? Who captures a runaway hit?
  5. Taiwan's domestic market cannot recover the cost of premium Taiwanese content either. Does the same mechanism apply here? If so, what is Taiwan's equivalent of MG, and who pays it?
  6. Would you use either of this essay's two numbers in a pitch deck? Under what caveat, and what would you go and check first?

In-class exercise — Payer / User Split Teardown

  1. Each team picks a content product they use weekly. Identify, separately: who consumes it, who pays for it, and at what moment the money is committed relative to consumption.
  2. Draw the selection rule. If payer and user differ, write the one question the funding party asks before saying yes. Make it specific enough to be answered with a number.
  3. Predict the drift. If that rule runs unchanged for five years, what does the catalogue look like? What genre or format disappears?
  4. Report out, one sentence per team: "Our product converges on ___ because ___ pays before ___ arrives."

The deliverable folds into the W3 Affordance Teardown homework as an added section: whose job is this product actually doing?

Transfer to the students' own project

The trap this case inoculates against, stated plainly for the W16 proposals: teams design for a user they interviewed, then fund the product from a party they never analysed. Require every final proposal to state its payer explicitly, name the evidence that payer will demand before committing, and say what the product will drift toward if that evidence becomes the selection rule. A team that cannot answer has designed a product with an unexamined author.
