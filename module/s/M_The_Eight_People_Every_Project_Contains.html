The Eight People Every Project Contains

This unit covers the eight identities that sit around any project, the difference between being responsible and being accountable, and the two frameworks that turn those identities into a decision your organisation can actually make.

🎯 Learning objectives

# What you will be able to do The real problem it lets you answer
1 Name the eight identities and say what each one can do to a project "Why did my proposal die after everyone said it was a good idea?" — usually because nobody asked the person who could veto it.
2 Separate responsible from accountable, and say why accountability cannot be split Two people were both "in charge" and when it failed each pointed at the other. That is not a personality problem; it is a design fault.
3 Choose between RACI and RAPID for a given situation "We already have a RACI chart, why is this decision still stuck?" — because RACI assigns tasks and this is a decision-rights problem.
4 Diagnose which identity is missing or doubled in a stalled project Every stalled project has one of three signatures: too many vetoes, no single decider, or a Consulted person who was handed a veto by accident.

The eight identities

Job titles tell you what someone does. These identities tell you what someone can do to you. They are a different axis, and you need both.

Identity Plain description What it controls
Sponsor The person who decides to give you resources Money, headcount, time
User The person who actually uses the product Whether any of it was worth doing
Approver The person who can veto it A stop, inside their own domain
Recommender The person who decides to start it and puts the proposal forward The quality of the options anyone else gets to choose from
Accountable / Decide The person who gets the credit if it works and the blame if it does not The final call
Responsible / Perform The person who does the work Whether it actually gets built
Informed The person who will hurt you if you do not report to them Political survival
Consulted / Input The person who gets angry if you do not ask them Whether your proposal rests on a gap

The last two are written in that deliberately blunt way because that is how they behave in real organisations.

Responsible and accountable are not the same word in a different register

The English words carry their own definitions. Responsible comes from respond — you did the work. Accountable comes from account, as in give an account — you have to answer for the result.

Responsible Accountable
The question it answers Who does the work Whose head is it on
Focus The task The outcome
How many people Can be many Exactly one
Can it be delegated The work can The accountability cannot
What they say afterwards "I finished my part" "It succeeded or failed on me"

Accountability disappears the moment you split it

If two people are both meant to answer for a result, then when it goes wrong each points at the other, and nobody answers at all. Responsibility divides without loss; accountability does not. This is why every framework below has a uniqueness rule at its centre.

Authority and resources must match the accountability

To hold someone accountable you have to give them decision rights and resources to match. Responsibility without authority is a scapegoat; authority without responsibility is an abuse. This is the most common organisational disease you will meet as a junior PM, and naming it is most of the defence.

For students · Three ways the balance fails

Lots of R, no A — many people working, nobody answering for the whole; everyone optimises their own piece and when it fails there is no one to ask. An A with weak or absent R — someone nominally carrying it with nobody actually doing the work, or an A who cannot delegate and becomes the bottleneck. More than one A — responsibility diluted until it evaporates, which returns you to the first case by a longer route.

Two frameworks, two different questions

The eight identities above are not one framework. They come from two, and using the wrong one is why some meetings never end.

RACI assigns tasks

Responsible (does the work, can be many) · Accountable (answers for it, exactly one) · Consulted (two-way, advises) · Informed (one-way, told afterwards).

The checking rule is mechanical: every row has exactly one A, at least one R, and not too many Cs and Is — because every C and I is a communication cost you have just signed up for. A blank cell is a normal, healthy answer.

RAPID assigns decision rights

Recommend (drives it, gathers evidence, proposes) · Agree (formal sign-off, which is a veto in practice) · Perform (does it once decided) · Input (consulted, no veto) · Decide (one person, final call).

The letters spell a word, not a sequence. The actual order is:

$$\text{Input} \rightarrow \text{Recommend} \rightarrow \text{Agree} \rightarrow \text{Decide} \rightarrow \text{Perform}$$

Use RACI for work, RAPID for decisions

RAPID RACI
What it governs Decision rights on one significant decision Execution of tasks and deliverables
Uniqueness rule One Decide One Accountable
Veto Agree holds one No such concept
Typical use A strategic choice: launch, acquire, allocate A project division-of-labour matrix

If a decision keeps stalling and you already have a RACI chart, that is the diagnosis: RACI has no way to express a veto, so nobody knows who holds one. See RACI matrix and RAPID decision framework for the full cards. They are two of six families of division-of-labour frameworks; the others cover how much authority to delegate, how to deliberately stop one person owning everything, and how much ceremony a decision is worth.

Each role has a specific failure mode

Recommend done lazily — the decider only ever sees bad options, so decision quality collapses at the source. Too many Agrees — everyone can block, nothing moves. A missing Agree — legal was not in the loop and the compliance mine goes off after launch. Perform brought in late — you ship a decision that cannot be executed. Input mistaken for Agree — a veto lands with someone who was only supposed to advise. More than one Decide — the decision splits and nobody owns it.

Job titles tell you what people do. These eight tell you what they can do to you — and the two identities students always forget, Approver and Consulted, are precisely the two that kill a proposal in week fifteen.

⚠️ Common misconceptions

What students assume Why it is wrong What to do instead
"Accountable and responsible are the same thing" One is doing, the other is answering. One can be shared, the other cannot Ask "whose head is it on" separately from "who does the work"
"The Sponsor and the Approver are the same person" Money and permission are usually held by different people List them separately every time, even when they turn out to be the same
"More people consulted means a better decision" Every C is a communication cost, and a C mistaken for an A becomes a veto Keep the Consulted list short and say out loud that they have no veto
"RACI covers everything" RACI has no way to express a veto or a single decider on a decision For a real decision, switch to RAPID
"The User is obviously included" The User is the identity most often present in the slides and absent from the room Ask who last spoke to one, and when

In short

  • A job title tells you what someone does; these eight identities tell you what someone can do to you, and only the second one predicts whether your project survives.
  • Accountability is single by definition — the moment two names share it, nobody holds it.
  • Use RACI when you are dividing work and RAPID when you are dividing decisions; mixing them is how projects end up with a decision everyone agreed to and nobody made.

💬 Discussion and reflection

Pair review

Take a project you have actually been on — an internship, a student organisation, a club event.

  1. Name the Sponsor, the Approver, and the User
  2. Name one person who was Consulted, and one who should have been but was not
  3. Say what went wrong because of the person in step 2
  4. Then say whether the problem was a task problem (RACI) or a decision problem (RAPID)

If neither of you can find a missing Consulted person, you have probably mislabelled the Approver.
