Algorithms of Oppression

A short primer on Algorithms of Oppression / Algorithmic Bias: what it claims, where it sits in this course, and the questions it lets you ask about your own product.

Safiya Umoja Noble (2018); Virginia Eubanks (2018); Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major & Mitchell (2021) — "Recommendation, ranking, and synthetic-data systems can amplify historical inequities."

The core idea

Algorithmic systems trained on historical data inherit and amplify the inequities of that history. The harms are not bugs at the margin — they are structural outputs of the design and training pipeline.

Key concepts: - Technological redlining (Noble) — search, ranking, and recommendation re-encode racialised exclusion - Allocative vs representational harm — denied resources vs distorted images of who people are - Stochastic parrots (Bender et al.) — large LMs encode and amplify training-data biases without understanding - Whose voice gets amplified when synthetic personas are generated by a biased model

Where this theory sits in this course

  • Supporting theory in W13 Synthetic Data & AI Persona — optional stretch material, and a useful contrasting lens in those weeks.

Lens questions

When you face a product decision, ask: 1. Whose historical data trained this model, and whose was missing? 2. Are the harms here allocative (denied service) or representational (distorted depiction) — or both? 3. When we generate synthetic users / data, whose voice gets amplified and whose is silenced? 4. What differential outcomes could this feature produce across groups — and have we measured that, not just average accuracy?

Pair with Agentic AI

Three concrete moves for AI-assisted PM work: 1. Audit synthetic personas / focus groups — whose perspective is over-represented, whose is missing; do not let "AI users" replace under-represented real users 2. Run an algorithmic-impact assessment before deploying any AI feature, with explicit attention to differential outcomes across groups 3. Hold the line on training-data provenance — what was scraped, who consented, who is paid, who is harmed; PMs are the upstream gate

⚠️ Pitfall: assuming "more data" fixes bias. Bender et al. show that scale often amplifies it — bigger training sets pull in more stereotype, not less.

Discussion

  1. Pick a synthetic-persona or AI-assisted research workflow. Whose voice does it amplify and whose does it silence — and how would you know?
  2. If an algorithmic-impact assessment surfaces a differential harm but the average metric improves, what authority does a PM actually have to halt the launch?
