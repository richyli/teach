Product-Market Fit

This unit covers the five elements a product has to answer to, the seven questions a value proposition must survive, and what happens to a content product when the person who uses it is not the person who pays for it.

🎯 Learning objectives

# What you will be able to do The real problem it lets you answer
1 Name the five elements — product, business environment, company, competition, user — and say what question each one asks Why does a proposal that everyone likes still fall apart? Because two of the five answers contradict each other and nobody checked.
2 Run Teece's seven questions against your own product idea and find the line you cannot fill in An empty line is not a gap in a form. It is a decision nobody has made, and it comes back as a change request during the build.
3 Separate the user from the payer, and predict which way the product will drift Japanese late-night anime is 90% dependent on overseas platforms, and the pre-sale price replaced editorial judgement as the rule for choosing what to make.
4 Judge whether a proposition is a solution or a novelty, and whether the cost of delivering it survives volume Generative AI makes the cost side of a content product volume-sensitive for the first time, and ad-supported models are the ones that break.

🤖 Pre-class AI workout

Use an AI as your study partner before class. Paste the prompt below.

Pick one subscription content product you personally pay for and one free content product you use daily. For each, answer these separately and do not blur them: who uses it, who pays for it, and what the paying party is actually buying. Then tell me one editorial or design decision in each product that only makes sense once you know who pays. Finally, name one decision in the free product that would be made differently if the users were the payers. Do not explain business models in general. Use these two products only.

Bring the free product's answer. The decision that would change if users paid is usually the one students find most uncomfortable, and it is the whole content of this unit.

A product is surrounded by four things it must answer to

The picture to keep is a ring: Product in the middle, with Business Environment, Company, Competition and User around it. Almost every argument you will lose this semester is an argument about one of those four rings, made in a document whose readers do not own that ring.

Element The question it asks of your product
Business Environment Is this a viable thing to be doing at all, given regulation, platforms, and the market's direction?
Company Can we do this, with what we have and what we are?
Competition Why us rather than the three organisations already doing something similar?
User Does anyone actually want it, in a way they would pay for or return to?
Product What is the thing, concretely?

Fit is a relationship, not a feature list

You do not achieve fit by adding features. You achieve it when the answers to those four questions stop contradicting each other. W3 to W7 walk around the ring one element at a time and assemble the answers into the MRD; W9 to W11 turn the settled answers into a specification.

For students · About the "faster horses" quote

You will see this attributed to Henry Ford everywhere: "If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses." There is no evidence Ford ever said it — researchers have looked, and it does not appear in his writings or interviews. Keep using it as a slogan about the limits of asking users to design, but do not use it as evidence, and notice what it is usually deployed to justify: ignoring users entirely. W6 gives you the Mom Test, which is the disciplined version of the same worry.

A firm cannot deliver value; it can only propose it

Vargo and Lusch's seventh foundational premise says the enterprise can only make value propositions. It has no ability to deliver value unilaterally: the most it can do is propose that its service will help someone reach a goal, and then wait for that person to bring their own resources and co-create the value.

That is not a philosophical nicety. It changes what a plan can promise. You cannot write "this feature will create value" into an MRD; you can only write what you are proposing and what the user would have to do for it to become value.

Seven questions your proposition has to survive

Teece (2010) sets out seven questions for examining a customer value proposition. They map onto the three mechanisms of a business model: creating value, delivering it, and capturing it. See Customer value proposition must answer multiple questions about use payment and segment size.

  1. How will the product or service be used, and how does it solve a customer's problem? (create)
  2. What might attract the customer to pay? (capture)
  3. How large is the target segment? (deliver)
  4. Is there already a competing offering? (create)
  5. Where is the industry in its evolution — has a dominant design emerged? (create)
  6. How should this be presented as a solution rather than a novelty? (create)
  7. What does it cost to deliver, and is that cost volume-sensitive? (capture)

For a founder this is the structure of a pitch deck. For you this semester it is the structure of the market section of your MRD.

Jobs to be done is question 1 with sharper teeth

Christensen's framing is that customers do not buy products, they hire them to make progress in a particular circumstance. Every job has functional, social, and emotional dimensions, and you have to see all three. The milkshake case is the standard illustration: the morning commuter hires a milkshake to make a dull commute survivable until lunch, and the same milkshake is hired on a Saturday afternoon by a father doing a completely different job — showing his child affection. Two jobs, one product. See jobs to be done framework.

Two ideas from it are worth carrying into your own research. Nonconsumption: the largest opportunity is often among people who have the job and no acceptable option, so they do nothing. Compensating behaviour: watching someone use a workaround to get a job done is a high-signal innovation cue.

In media, the user and the payer are usually different people

Picard's dual product market: a news organisation sells content to an audience and simultaneously sells that audience's attention to advertisers, so its revenue depends on two parties at once. Since the 1950s the dependence has tilted towards advertising, and online news is the extreme case — free content to attract traffic, revenue almost entirely from ads. See dual product market.

When the payer is not the user, the product drifts towards the payer

This is the structural fact behind a large share of the complaints people make about media products. Nobody has to be cynical for it to happen: the payer's preferences are the ones with a feedback loop attached.

The W3 case makes it concrete. In Japanese late-night anime, roughly nine-tenths of the revenue depends on overseas platforms, and the minimum-guarantee pre-sale price came to function as the rule for deciding what gets made at all — displacing editorial judgement. Running the Value Proposition Canvas twice on the same work, once for the viewer and once for the platform, produces two different products. See Case - Anime Revenue Structure and Isekai Saturation.

Willingness to pay is not fixed, and it is not only about money

Research on paying for news finds a payer mentality that extends across commercial and public media: people who already pay for commercial media are more likely to pay for public service media too, which argues against the assumption that these compete for a fixed pot. See Payer mentality extends across commercial and public media. If you are proposing a paid content product, your addressable market is not "people with money"; it is people already in the habit of paying for something like it.

Generative AI moves the cost question from question 7 to question 1

For most of the history of digital content, delivering one more copy cost nothing, so question 7 was close to trivial. That is no longer true. BuzzFeed's engineering team reported that the unit economics of hosted LLM services put real pressure on already-thin advertising margins: every generation is a direct cost, and an ad-supported model cannot pass it on the way a subscription or transactional product can. Their conclusion was that cost control has to be a first-class product design question rather than a later optimisation. See Hosted LLM unit economics strain ad-supported media business models.

Fit is not the moment users like your product. It is the moment your answers about the environment, the company, the competition and the user stop contradicting each other — and the answer that contradicts most often is the one about who pays.

⚠️ Common misconceptions

What students assume Why it is wrong What to do instead
"Product–market fit means users like it" Liking it is one of five answers. Fit is the five answers being consistent Write all five and look for the contradiction
"Add features until it fits" Features are the product ring only. A fit problem is usually in one of the other four Ask which ring the objection came from
"We create value for users" Vargo and Lusch's FP7: a firm can only propose value; the user co-creates it or it does not happen Write what you propose and what the user has to do
"Our users are our market" In media the payer is often a third party, and the product drifts towards whoever pays Write user and payer as two separate lines, always
"The faster-horses quote proves you should ignore users" The quote is almost certainly apocryphal, and even at face value it argues against asking users to design, not against researching them Use it as a warning about question design, then learn the Mom Test in W6
"Delivery cost is a finance problem, not mine" With generative AI in the product, cost is volume-sensitive and belongs in the design Answer question 7 before you commit to the feature

Individual reflection
