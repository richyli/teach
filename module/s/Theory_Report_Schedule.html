Theory Report Schedule — Content Product Planning 2026

This page covers which theory belongs to which week, what a Theory Report has to contain, and where to find the primer for every theory on the list.

Every week from W3 to W15 opens with a student report

A 15-minute student-led report opens the session. There are 21 slots across W3–W15 — one for every theory on the list except Activity Theory, which we covered in W1. Supporting Theories are reported too, and no theory is reported twice. W8 and W16 are assessment weeks and carry no report.

Pick your slot here: Theory Report sign-up sheet. One slot per person. Four slots take two people because their founding texts are long — Surveillance Capitalism (W3), Sensemaking (W4), Actor-Network Theory (W14), Distributed Cognition (W15). Everything else is a solo report.

What the report has to contain

Four things, then two questions:

  1. The original source — who said it, when, in what argument
  2. The core mechanism — one sentence beginning "this theory says…" that the rest of the class can quote afterwards
  3. One classic empirical study — the claim, the method, the finding
  4. One substantive criticism — a real objection from the literature, not "it is hard to measure"

Then end with two discussion prompts that connect the theory to that week's product topic and that people can actually disagree about.

The report is graded on five dimensions

Dimension What a 4 looks like
Source fidelity The original text is represented accurately; key terms are used the way the author defines them
Mechanism clarity The core concept lands as a one-sentence claim classmates can quote
Empirical anchor + critique One classic study summarised (claim + method + finding) and one substantive criticism articulated
Discussion-prompt quality Two prompts that genuinely link the theory to the week's product topic and provoke disagreement
Delivery Within 15 minutes, paced, no reading from slides, one well-chosen example

Each dimension is scored 1–4. Your one report is worth 20% of the course grade, so there is no second attempt to average it out.

The session runs as six stages, so the room is graded too

Pre-class question submission → the 15-minute talk → an AI translator round → a red-team challenge → a theory-in-use map → a one-line synthesis. If you are not presenting, your contribution in stages 3 to 6 is what your participation mark is made of.

Twenty-one of the twenty-two theories carry a report

Activity Theory is the exception — we covered it in W1, instructor-led. Every other theory gets exactly one slot, whether the table below lists it as Primary or as Supporting. A Supporting frame is there to disagree with the primary lens rather than to reinforce it, which is why it now carries a report of its own.

Three theories appear twice in the table. Affordance Theory returns in W3 and W9 because a wireframe and a content product are the same problem at two resolutions. Boundary Object Theory carries from W7 to W11, so you meet the MRD and the PRD as two contrasting boundary objects in one semester. Sensemaking anchors W4 and W15, market research and risk being the same interpretive work pointed in opposite directions. The report goes with the first appearance; in W9, W11 and W15 the slot goes to a Supporting Theory instead, so the week keeps its lens but the report brings a second one.

Putting a theory into a prompt

An AI will summarise any theory on request. That is the least useful thing it can do with one, and it is what most reports end up quoting. The prompts below do the other three things: make the theory explain something, make it do work on a case, and make it change a product decision.

Use them in order. Each one takes the output of the last. Replace the bracketed parts and paste the rest verbatim — the constraints at the end of each prompt are doing most of the work.

1. Explain — force it to teach, not to define

I am preparing a 15-minute report on [THEORY] for a product-planning course. Do not give me a definition or a summary. Instead: (1) state the single mechanism this theory claims, in one sentence beginning "this theory says", using no term the theory itself invented; (2) give me the one classic empirical study that tested it, with its claim, its method and its finding; (3) give me the strongest published criticism of it, from a named critic, not a generic limitation; (4) tell me the one thing people most often get wrong when they cite this theory. Then ask me two questions I probably cannot answer yet.

Then push back once:

Now argue that the mechanism you just described is trivially true — that it dresses up something obvious. Make the strongest version of that attack. Then tell me what the theory would have to predict for the attack to fail.

If a theory survives that second prompt with something specific, you have found what to report. If it does not, you have found your critique section.

2. Apply — make it explain one concrete case

Here is a real case: [PASTE 150 WORDS ABOUT A REAL PRODUCT, INCIDENT, OR DECISION]. Using only [THEORY], explain what is happening. Rules: name which element or construct of the theory each part of the case maps onto; mark every place where the mapping is forced, and say so out loud rather than smoothing it over; then name one thing in this case that the theory cannot explain at all. Do not tell me the theory is useful. Show me where it runs out.

The last instruction is the one that matters. A theory that explains everything in your case has been applied loosely, and the marker for that in a report is that nothing was left over.

Then swap lenses:

Now explain the same case using [THIS WEEK'S SUPPORTING THEORY]. Give me the two sentences where the two explanations genuinely disagree — not where they use different words for the same thing.

3. Plan — make it change a product decision

I am planning [YOUR PRODUCT] and I have to decide [THE SPECIFIC DECISION: what to build first, who to research, how to price, what to cut]. Use [THEORY] to make this decision differently from how I would make it by instinct. Give me: (1) the question the theory forces me to ask before deciding; (2) what evidence I would have to collect to answer it; (3) the decision the theory points to, and the decision it points away from; (4) what would have to be true for the theory to be the wrong lens here. Do not give me a balanced overview. Commit to a recommendation.

Then check whether the theory did any work at all:

Take your recommendation and rewrite it with every reference to [THEORY] deleted. If the recommendation still reads the same, tell me plainly — that means the theory was decorative, not load-bearing.

That final check is the same test the rubric applies to your report and to your pitch: a theory that could be deleted without changing the argument was never used.

What to bring back to class

From prompt 1, the criticism and the thing people get wrong. From prompt 2, the part of the case the theory cannot explain. From prompt 3, the answer to the deletion test. Those three are the parts of an AI conversation that survive contact with a room full of people who have read the same theory.

How to prepare, in the order that works

Read the primer page for your theory first — it gives you the claim, the lens questions, and the founding readings. Then read one founding text; the primer is not a substitute and a report built only on the primer is visible immediately. Then find your empirical study and your criticism, because those two are what separate a 4 from a 3. Write the two discussion prompts last, once you know what the theory cannot explain about this week's product topic — that gap is where the good prompts are.

Use an AI to stress-test the report before you give it: ask it to argue that your theory does not apply to this week's topic at all, and prepare an answer. Somebody in the red-team round will make that argument anyway.
